AI sped up coding. Review is paying the bill.

AI is changing how code gets written, but not always how it gets delivered. The bottleneck is shifting from typing faster to reviewing, aligning, and cleaning up what the tools produce.
01
Story 01 of 04
 

The speed gain is real, but it’s getting absorbed
That means the personal win can be real while the team-level win stays modest.

Most developers can feel the difference when an assistant helps them draft code, scaffold a function, or fill in repetitive pieces. The catch is that the time saved at the keyboard is often disappearing later in the workflow. Teams are spending more effort checking generated code, fixing edge cases, and untangling changes that looked fine on first pass. If your process still assumes every change is hand-written, you may be trading one kind of friction for another. The faster path is only useful if the rest of the system can keep up.

Code review is becoming the new time sink
Reviewers now have to verify logic, spot mismatched assumptions, and confirm that the implementation fits the broader system, not just the local repo.

The newest failure mode is not that AI writes bad code all the time. It is that it writes plausible code fast enough to create a bigger review pile. That is especially painful when the change crosses services or touches shared data models. If your team is seeing more AI usage, expect review standards to tighten, not loosen. A useful habit is to make review checklists more explicit, so people are not re-litigating the same issues on every pull request.

Skipping design review saves minutes, then costs hours
But for changes that span multiple services, that shortcut often hides architectural assumptions that should have been surfaced earlier.

When a request goes straight from product notes to code, it can feel efficient in the moment. A lightweight design review does not need to be a heavy committee meeting. It just needs enough structure to answer a few basic questions: What systems are affected? What are the failure modes? Which parts are risky enough to deserve extra scrutiny? If agents are writing more of the first draft, the design conversation becomes more important, not less. The goal is to catch bad shape before it becomes expensive code review.

Story 04 of 04
 

AI strategy matters more than AI enthusiasm
It means deciding where AI is welcome, where it needs review, and what quality bar applies before code lands.

One of the clearest patterns is that teams with a defined approach to AI tend to have a better experience than teams that just let everyone improvise. That does not mean a giant policy doc or a centralized gatekeeper. The same logic applies to management. If AI changes how work is done, managers spend less time supervising raw output and more time setting constraints, clarifying ownership, and removing avoidable friction. The teams that benefit most will be the ones that pair faster drafting with better operating rules.
