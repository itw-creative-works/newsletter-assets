Stop treating organic traffic like a vanity metric
What it can do is move someone closer to buying, answering a question early, earning trust, or bringing them back later through search.

Traffic is nice. Revenue is better. The gap between the two is where most SEO reporting gets awkward, because a blog post rarely closes a deal on its own. That means you need a way to assign value to the assist, not just the last click. If you are only measuring visits, you are grading the loudest work, not the most useful work. The practical move is to tie content to outcomes you already care about: signups, demo requests, purchases, or qualified replies.

Use page value to price the invisible work
In plain English: your help article, comparison page, or landing page may not collect money itself, but it may be doing the heavy lifting before the money shows up.

Not every page should be judged by direct sales. Some pages do the unglamorous work of helping a visitor figure out what to do next. That is where page value comes in. It gives non-transactional pages a monetary weight based on sessions that eventually led to a conversion. This is especially useful for e-commerce and lead-gen teams that want a cleaner read on content performance. If a page consistently appears before conversions, it deserves credit instead of a shrug.

Track SEO on a monthly cadence or stay confused
At that cadence, you can compare cost against outcome, see which topics are climbing, and notice when rankings are not translating into useful actions.

SEO is slow enough without sloppy measurement. A monthly review gives you enough data to spot trends without chasing noise from one weird week. It also keeps the conversation honest inside the team. Instead of saying, 'content is doing well,' you can say, 'this cluster brought in the right visitors, this one did not, and here is what it cost.' That is the kind of sentence that survives a budget meeting. If you want better decisions, measure in the same rhythm you plan in.
