Agentic coding is powerful, but too often private
That is useful, but it also creates a bad default: one person gives the prompt, one person reviews the output, and everyone else stays out of the loop.

The big shift in AI coding is not just faster autocomplete. It is the move from conversation to execution, where a model can plan, edit, test, and submit work with a computer in the loop. When that happens, teams do not become more collaborative. They become more fragmented. The best use of these tools is not to replace coordination, but to reduce the amount of busywork that blocks it. If your agent is only visible to one developer, you are leaving most of the upside on the table.

The review problem is the real production risk
If an AI can draft a patch in minutes, the review step should become more intentional, not less.

Speed is easy to measure. Trust is not. The dangerous pattern in agentic coding is when the same developer both creates and signs off on the change, because that removes the second set of eyes that catches subtle bugs, design regressions, and security issues. Teams should treat agent output like a junior contributor’s work: useful, but never exempt from scrutiny. Strong orgs will track defect rates, rollback frequency, and review depth alongside throughput. If you only count lines shipped, you will miss the cost of rushing bad code into production.

AI budgets are rising faster than confidence
For engineering leaders, this means the conversation is shifting from experimentation to accountability.

There is a widening gap between what companies spend on AI and what they can prove it is delivering. That is a familiar pattern in new infrastructure: early enthusiasm drives adoption, then finance starts asking harder questions. The right question is not whether your team uses AI. It is whether the tool shortens meaningful work, improves quality, or just increases activity. If you cannot connect spend to outcomes, the budget will get attacked eventually. The companies that win will be the ones that instrument usage, tie it to delivery metrics, and cut the parts that do not move the business.

Use AI to increase leverage, not isolation
AI should compress the boring parts of work so humans can spend more time making decisions together.

The best AI workflows do not end with a solo developer and a generated diff. They start there and then move into shared habits: pair review, reusable prompts, team templates, and regular postmortems on what the agent got wrong. That is how you turn one person’s productivity gain into organizational learning. The same applies beyond code. Whether you are researching, drafting, or building internal tools, the goal is to make the process more legible to the team, not less. If it is not creating that kind of leverage, it is probably just creating more private speed.
