# AI models are getting weirder at writing

_A new DeepSeek release, plus a reality check on AI text quality_

This week’s update is less about flashy AI demos and more about what students actually feel when they use these tools: the output can change a lot depending on the setting, and writing quality is still a mixed bag.

That matters if you use AI to study, outline essays, or explain tough topics, because the best results still come from asking for help in a specific, controlled way.

---

## Same prompt, different answer

![A student comparing three AI-generated answer bubbles on a laptop screen, clean editorial illustration, bright classroom style.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/studymonkey/content/-OztWkhi3-88TmfDbzNm/section-1.png)

One of the more interesting signs of progress is also a reminder to be careful: the same request can lead to very different responses when you change how much the model is “thinking.” In one test, even a simple image prompt produced clearly different versions across low, medium, and high reasoning modes. For students, that means the first answer is not always the best answer. If you’re using StudyMonkey or any other tutor tool, try asking for a shorter version first, then a step-by-step breakdown, then a final check. That gives you more control over whether you want speed, detail, or accuracy.

## AI writing still struggles with voice

![A notebook with a half-written essay beside a laptop showing an outline and highlighted phrases, warm realistic style.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/studymonkey/content/-OztWkhi3-88TmfDbzNm/section-2.png)

AI is getting better at explaining facts, but it still has trouble sounding genuinely human when the writing needs a point of view. That shows up most in essays, reflections, and anything where the writer’s reasoning matters as much as the conclusion. For homework, that is actually useful to know. If you ask AI to write your full response, you may get something polished but oddly flat. A better approach is to use it for the boring part first: outlines, topic sentences, examples, or a rough draft you can reshape. Then add your own details, class notes, and opinions so the final version sounds like you, not a generic bot.

## Why this matters for studying

![A student using an AI tutor on a phone while taking notes in a spiral notebook, friendly modern study scene.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/studymonkey/content/-OztWkhi3-88TmfDbzNm/section-3.png)

The best homework use of AI is not replacing your thinking. It is speeding up the parts that usually slow you down. Need help with algebra? Ask for one worked example, then a second problem with the steps hidden so you can try it yourself. Writing an essay? Ask for a thesis check, then a list of claims and counterclaims. Reviewing for a test? Ask for flashcards, then quiz yourself without looking. The tool is most helpful when it gives you a clearer path, not when it does the entire climb for you. That keeps the learning active, which is where the real grade boost comes from.

## The practical takeaway for AI tutors

![Close-up of a student refining an AI tutor prompt on a phone, with sticky notes and a textbook in frame, crisp digital illustration.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/studymonkey/content/-OztWkhi3-88TmfDbzNm/section-4.png)

As models get more capable, the smartest users will not be the ones who ask the broadest questions. They will be the ones who know how to steer the tool. That means giving context, asking for one task at a time, and checking the result against your assignment instructions. If the answer feels too vague, ask for an example. If it feels too fancy, ask for simpler wording. If it feels incomplete, request the missing steps. A good tutor should make the work easier to understand, not harder to trust.
