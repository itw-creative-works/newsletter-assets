Automate the annoying 1%, not the whole day

The smartest automation I keep seeing is not a giant AI agent fantasy. It is a string of small, trusted shortcuts that remove the same annoyances from your day over and over.

That is the real win: less context switching, fewer approvals by hand, and more time spent on the work that actually needs judgment.

Start with the bottleneck, not the wishlist
A short briefing on pending approvals, a reminder to follow up on stuck requests, or a digest of what needs attention can save more time than a flashy all-purpose agent.

If you want better automation, don’t begin with the dream of a fully autonomous assistant. Begin with the task that irritates you every four hours, every morning, or every time you open Slack. That is usually where the leverage lives. The point is not to automate your whole role. It is to remove the smallest repeatable drag first, then look for the next one. Once a single annoying loop disappears, the rest of your workflow becomes easier to spot.

Some code should skip review, on purpose
Small UI tweaks often move faster when you trust the engineer, ship it, and polish it in production with real feedback.

Not every pull request deserves the same amount of ceremony. The same goes for early experiments that might be thrown away tomorrow. Review is valuable, but it can become a tax when the change is low risk or still taking shape. A better rule is to reserve heavy review for the parts that can break systems, affect coordination, or need senior judgment. For everything else, give people ownership, clear guardrails, and permission to iterate. That is how you raise throughput without turning the codebase into a free-for-all.

Trustworthy automation needs guardrails, not vibes
That means clear permission boundaries, specialist sub-tasks, and a way to explain what happened after the fact.

The useful version of automation is not the one that does everything. It is the one that can act on your behalf without creating new messes. If a system can order lunch, book a table, or send a follow-up, fine, but it should also know where it is allowed to stop and ask. The same principle applies to work tools. You want speed, but you also want reversibility, visibility, and a narrow blast radius when something goes wrong. Trust is built by constraints, not by making the system sound smarter.

Keyboard-first tools make the little wins stick
They disappear into your flow and keep the work moving without forcing another tab, another menu, or another retype.

The best productivity tools do not make you think about the tool. They make you move faster through the work. Keyboard access matters because it cuts friction on the tasks you repeat all day: composing replies, opening sidebars, jumping between panels, inserting snippets, and filling in standard text. The same is true for snippet libraries. If the shortcut is easy to remember and the content is clean, people actually use it. That is why the best systems feel almost boring in the best way possible.
