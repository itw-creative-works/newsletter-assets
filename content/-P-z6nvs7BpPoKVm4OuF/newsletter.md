# Stop solving only the obvious problem

_A better snippet mindset: shape the system, and let juniors build judgment first._

The pattern showing up in engineering teams is pretty clear: speed is up, but so is the risk of optimizing the wrong thing. That changes how we think about senior impact, junior growth, and the kind of work worth repeating.

---

## The highest-leverage fix is the one you do once

![A focused engineer at a desk looking at a recurring workflow diagram, with arrows collapsing into a cleaner system map, modern editorial illustration style.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P-z6nvs7BpPoKVm4OuF/section-1.png)

A lot of engineers get rewarded for being the person who can jump in, patch the issue, and move on. That matters. But the bigger win is often upstream: finding the pattern behind the bug, the bottleneck, or the repeated support ask and changing the system so it stops coming back. The best senior work tends to make you less necessary over time, not more. That might mean reworking a query path, tightening a deployment step, or turning a messy tribal process into a shared snippet or playbook. If the same problem keeps landing in your lap, the real task is usually not faster typing. It is better design.

## Junior engineers need judgment, not just output

![A junior developer in a calm training environment looking at layered system diagrams, with a mentor nearby and subtle AI suggestions on a side panel, clean flat illustration.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P-z6nvs7BpPoKVm4OuF/section-2.png)

When code gets generated quickly, it is tempting to judge progress by how much landed in the repo. That misses the more important question: does the engineer understand what they built, why it works, and where it might fail? Early-career people still need real practice tracing systems, choosing where to debug first, and making decisions under pressure. The goal is not to keep them slow. It is to give them enough structure that their speed comes with understanding. A strong apprenticeship should gradually remove training wheels, not skip the learning part entirely. Otherwise, teams end up with people who can accept suggestions but cannot yet defend a fix.

## Use AI like scaffolding, not autopilot

![A three-step ladder labeled tutor, copilot, accelerator beside a developer workspace, with AI assistance becoming lighter at each step, crisp infographic style.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P-z6nvs7BpPoKVm4OuF/section-3.png)

AI tools are useful when they act like a tutor first, then a copilot, then an accelerator. That sequence matters. Early on, the tool should explain and guide. As skill grows, it can pair with the engineer. Only later should it speed up work that the engineer already understands well. The risk is letting the machine do the thinking before the person knows how to think through the problem themselves. In practice, that means using AI for drafts, checks, and exploration, while still forcing a human to own the diagnosis. Good teams do not just measure output. They also watch for learning, comprehension, and judgment getting stronger over time.

## Make your snippets reflect the system, not the symptom

![A snippet library interface with folders for incident updates, debugging, and handoffs, shown on laptop and phone screens syncing together, sleek product illustration.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P-z6nvs7BpPoKVm4OuF/section-4.png)

This is where a good snippet library earns its keep. The best reusable text is not just the response that saves a minute today. It is the template that helps the team avoid repeating the same confusion tomorrow. Think incident updates, debugging checklists, handoff notes, and customer replies that encode the reason behind the action. The more your snippets capture judgment, the less you rely on memory when the pressure is on. That is especially useful for support teams and engineers who move between devices and contexts all day. A few well-written snippets can turn hard-won experience into something the rest of the team can reuse instantly.
