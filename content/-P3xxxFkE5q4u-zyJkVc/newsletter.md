# AI agents changed the job. So did the stack.

_Models, apps, and harnesses now matter more than chat alone._

The big change in AI right now is not another chatbot release. It is the move from asking questions to assigning work, which forces teams to think in terms of models, apps, and the systems that keep agents useful without letting them wander.

---

## Stop choosing AI like it is still a chatbot

![A clean technical illustration of three stacked layers labeled model, app, and harness, with an AI agent moving from question answering to task execution.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/optiic/content/-P3xxxFkE5q4u-zyJkVc/section-1.png)

If you are still picking tools by asking “which chatbot is smartest,” you are already behind the curve. The practical decision now has three layers. First is the model, which drives reasoning, writing, coding, and multimodal performance. Second is the app, which determines how well you can actually use that model to get work done. Third is the harness, meaning the workflow, permissions, and guardrails around the agent. In real teams, the app and harness often matter more than raw model quality because they shape whether the tool stays useful after the first demo. The fastest teams are not worshipping one model. They are matching the tool to the job.

## The new leverage comes from management, not coding alone

![A founder and operator team building a startup prototype at a whiteboard, with AI tools on screen generating screens, research notes, and pitch deck drafts.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/optiic/content/-P3xxxFkE5q4u-zyJkVc/section-2.png)

One of the clearest signs that AI is reshaping work is how far non-programmers can get with the right setup. Give smart operators decent agent tools, and they can move from idea to prototype, then from prototype to market analysis, pricing logic, and pitch materials without waiting on a full engineering cycle. That does not mean coding stops mattering. It means coding becomes one part of a broader execution system. The new advantage is managerial judgment: deciding what to build, what to ignore, and how to package a good enough prototype into something real. In other words, AI rewards people who can direct work, not just produce it.

## When code gets cheap, accountability gets expensive

![A busy software team reviewing a large pull request on multiple monitors, with one engineer clearly taking ownership while an AI assistant suggests changes in the background.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/optiic/content/-P3xxxFkE5q4u-zyJkVc/section-3.png)

As AI writes more of the code, the bottleneck shifts from typing faster to judging better. That sounds abstract until you are the one signing off on a thousand-line change, a big migration, or a Friday afternoon release. Fast-moving teams are finding that velocity only works when ownership is explicit. Every AI-assisted change still needs a human who understands the tradeoffs, can explain the intent, and is willing to carry the pager if it breaks. The healthiest engineering cultures are the ones that treat AI as a multiplier, not a shield. Speed is real, but so is the need for discipline, review, and personal responsibility.

## FDEs win by learning the business, not hoarding expertise

![A forward-deployed engineer sitting with a customer team, mapping a business workflow on a glass wall while laptop screens show APIs and system diagrams.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/optiic/content/-P3xxxFkE5q4u-zyJkVc/section-4.png)

Forward-deployed engineering is becoming less about knowing every framework and more about knowing where the problem lives. The best people in this role are part builder, part translator, part operator. They can work in Python, SQL, APIs, and agent tooling when needed, but their real edge is understanding the customer’s process well enough to shape the right solution and ask the right expert when they hit a wall. That matters because many AI projects fail not on model capability, but on vague requirements and weak integration with how the business actually runs. If you want to be valuable here, get closer to the workflow, not just the stack.

## Shared agents need permissions, not just prompts

![A collaboration workspace with a shared AI agent in a team channel, surrounded by permission icons, approval checkmarks, and audit logs in a modern product UI style.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/optiic/content/-P3xxxFkE5q4u-zyJkVc/section-5.png)

The next wave of agents is moving into shared workspaces, where teams tag one agent to start tasks in Slack, Teams, or other collaboration tools. That is powerful, but it creates a permissions problem immediately. If anyone can ask the agent to act, whose authority does it use? What can it read, change, or approve? The answer is not to lock everything down until the agent is useless. It is to define clear thresholds: low-risk tasks can move fast, while anything consequential should pause for human review. The winning teams will design agent access like they design production systems: least privilege, obvious boundaries, and auditability built in from day one.
