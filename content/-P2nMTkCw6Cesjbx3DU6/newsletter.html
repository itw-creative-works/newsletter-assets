AI sped up shipping, not understanding

AI has changed the pace of software delivery, but it has not changed one basic requirement: someone still has to explain what happened when things go wrong.

That is where a lot of teams are getting exposed.
01
Story 01 of 04
 

Fast delivery can hide a slow diagnosis
It is about preserving enough context that the next person can reconstruct the decision trail without starting from scratch.

A change can pass review, clear QA, and roll back cleanly, yet still leave the team guessing about why it failed. That is the new blind spot. The work is no longer just about shipping safely. If your team can move quickly but cannot explain the failure path in plain language, your process is only half working. Speed is useful. Speed plus understanding is what keeps the same issue from coming back in a new wrapper.

Reopened work is a signal, not a nuisance
When merge requests keep reopening, rollbacks turn into arguments, or decisions get pushed upward because the team is unsure, those are not minor annoyances.

They are clues that the system is producing changes faster than it is producing shared understanding. Support teams see this when a canned reply is polished but the underlying issue is still fuzzy. Engineering teams see it when the fix lands but nobody can explain the failure mode. The smarter move is to treat repeated back-and-forth as operational debt and tighten the loop where context is getting lost.

Don’t build a workflow around the expert who remembers everything
If one engineer leaves, or even just goes on vacation, the team should not have to rebuild the reasoning from scratch.

AI assistants can help draft code, summarize threads, and speed up routine work. The trap is assuming that means knowledge is safely stored. Often, the real understanding still lives in one or two people’s heads, plus a trail of prompts and half-forgotten context. This is a good moment to capture better snippets, decision notes, incident summaries, and rollback reasons in a place the whole team can reuse. Shared context is a workflow feature, not a nice-to-have.

04
Story 04 of 04
 

Measure comprehension, not just completion
That is why a useful review process should include a simple question: if this broke again tomorrow, could a different person describe why?

Completion metrics tell you the change shipped. They do not tell you whether the team can explain it later. If the answer is no, the issue is not just technical. It is a knowledge transfer problem. Teams that rely on text snippets, postmortem templates, and reusable incident notes tend to recover faster because the reasoning is already captured in a form people can actually use. The goal is not more documentation. The goal is usable memory.
