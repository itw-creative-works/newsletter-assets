# AI sped up shipping, not understanding

_Healthy dashboards can hide a team’s weakest link: explaining what broke._

AI has changed the pace of software delivery, but it has not changed one basic requirement: someone still has to explain what happened when things go wrong.

That is where a lot of teams are getting exposed.

---

## Fast delivery can hide a slow diagnosis

![A modern engineering dashboard on one screen and a team huddled around a whiteboard on the other, clean editorial illustration, muted blues and grays.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P2nMTkCw6Cesjbx3DU6/section-1.png)

A change can pass review, clear QA, and roll back cleanly, yet still leave the team guessing about why it failed. That is the new blind spot. The work is no longer just about shipping safely. It is about preserving enough context that the next person can reconstruct the decision trail without starting from scratch. If your team can move quickly but cannot explain the failure path in plain language, your process is only half working. Speed is useful. Speed plus understanding is what keeps the same issue from coming back in a new wrapper.

## Reopened work is a signal, not a nuisance

![A stack of reopened pull requests and sticky notes on a laptop, visualized as an organized but strained workflow, flat vector style.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P2nMTkCw6Cesjbx3DU6/section-2.png)

When merge requests keep reopening, rollbacks turn into arguments, or decisions get pushed upward because the team is unsure, those are not minor annoyances. They are clues that the system is producing changes faster than it is producing shared understanding. Support teams see this when a canned reply is polished but the underlying issue is still fuzzy. Engineering teams see it when the fix lands but nobody can explain the failure mode. The smarter move is to treat repeated back-and-forth as operational debt and tighten the loop where context is getting lost.

## Don’t build a workflow around the expert who remembers everything

![An engineer leaving a desk while teammates access a shared internal knowledge library on multiple devices, warm editorial illustration.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P2nMTkCw6Cesjbx3DU6/section-3.png)

AI assistants can help draft code, summarize threads, and speed up routine work. The trap is assuming that means knowledge is safely stored. Often, the real understanding still lives in one or two people’s heads, plus a trail of prompts and half-forgotten context. If one engineer leaves, or even just goes on vacation, the team should not have to rebuild the reasoning from scratch. This is a good moment to capture better snippets, decision notes, incident summaries, and rollback reasons in a place the whole team can reuse. Shared context is a workflow feature, not a nice-to-have.

---

## Measure comprehension, not just completion

![A postmortem template beside a keyboard with reusable snippets highlighted, crisp product-style illustration with one warm accent color.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/sniips/content/-P2nMTkCw6Cesjbx3DU6/section-4.png)

Completion metrics tell you the change shipped. They do not tell you whether the team can explain it later. That is why a useful review process should include a simple question: if this broke again tomorrow, could a different person describe why? If the answer is no, the issue is not just technical. It is a knowledge transfer problem. Teams that rely on text snippets, postmortem templates, and reusable incident notes tend to recover faster because the reasoning is already captured in a form people can actually use. The goal is not more documentation. The goal is usable memory.
