# Google Ads now lets you steer budget by channel

_New priority controls could change how marketers split spend across Google inventory._

Google is giving advertisers a more manual hand on the wheel. That matters because when you are sending offline traffic from a flyer, menu, or package into a paid landing page, where the budget lands can shape what happens after the scan.

---

## Budget control is getting more granular

![A clean editorial illustration of a marketer adjusting a dashboard with stacked budget bars, showing high and low priority channels in a modern ad interface.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/dashqr/content/-P3vSjF3tmDWj6-Z2IYl/section-1.png)

The new setup gives advertisers two useful levers: rank a channel as higher priority, or keep lower-priority inventory on a tighter leash. Instead of turning channels off completely, marketers can now tell Google where to spend first and where to spend only if budget remains. That is a practical shift for teams that want reach without letting spend drift into placements that do not convert as well. For QR-driven campaigns, this kind of control is helpful when one landing page needs serious demand capture while another is meant to support awareness or retargeting.

## Search, Shopping, PMax, and Demand Gen all get touched

![An infographic-style scene with four campaign tiles labeled Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and Demand Gen connected to a shared budget dashboard.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/dashqr/content/-P3vSjF3tmDWj6-Z2IYl/section-2.png)

This is not a single-campaign tweak. The rollout reaches Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and Demand Gen, which means the change can affect a wide slice of paid search and cross-channel buying. In some campaign types, Google-owned inventory is bundled together, so the level of control is not identical everywhere. That makes campaign structure even more important. If a brand uses a QR code on packaging or in-store signage, the post-scan path should line up with the campaign type behind it, because not every setup will let you separate channels the same way.

## Use it to match intent to destination

![A split-screen illustration of two QR code campaigns, one for fast ordering and one for lead capture, each feeding into different landing pages with priority arrows.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/dashqr/content/-P3vSjF3tmDWj6-Z2IYl/section-3.png)

The real value here is matching spend to intent. A restaurant menu QR might point to a same-day ordering page, while a product insert QR could drive to a higher-consideration offer like a demo or signup. With priority controls, you can protect the budget behind the highest-intent path first, then let secondary inventory absorb the rest. That is a better fit than treating all scan traffic the same. It also makes A/B testing more meaningful, because you can compare not just landing pages, but the quality of traffic flowing into each one.

## Dynamic QR campaigns still need measurement discipline

![A branded QR code on a product box leading to a mobile analytics dashboard, shown in a polished flat-lay marketing scene.](https://cdn.itwcreativeworks.com/newsletters/dashqr/content/-P3vSjF3tmDWj6-Z2IYl/section-4.png)

More control over media spend does not fix weak tracking. If the page behind the QR code is slow, vague, or hard to convert on mobile, better budget allocation will not rescue the campaign. Teams should still watch scan volume, landing page conversion, and downstream actions side by side. The best use of these new controls is to reduce wasted spend while keeping the funnel aligned from scan to sale. In practice, that means pairing a branded QR code with clear analytics, a fast mobile page, and a campaign structure that reflects what you actually want people to do next.
